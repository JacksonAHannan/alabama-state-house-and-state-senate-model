# James C. Fields Jr.

## Why he matters

James Fields recorded about `+15.6` out-of-fold CMO as the Democratic incumbent in House District 12 in 2010. He lost by roughly eight margin points, but the same-cycle Democratic top-ticket baseline in the district was approximately negative 43. His raw legislative overperformance was therefore about 34 points.

Fields is analytically valuable because he was a Black Democrat representing an overwhelmingly white Cullman County district during the Republican wave. His case tests whether a personal vote rooted in local, occupational, and religious identity could cross both partisan and racial boundaries.

## Campaign-time ideological evidence

Fields's pre-election Shor–McCarty score was `-0.131`, around the 64th conservative percentile of the contemporary Alabama Democratic House caucus. That places him mildly on the caucus's conservative side, but not near its extreme.

His issue profile was not uniformly conservative. Official January 2009 Cullman County Commission minutes record Fields saying education funding required immediate attention and advocating immediate passage of a half-cent sales tax for schools, followed by a referendum in 2010. Relative to Alabama Democrats, this supports a mildly progressive public-investment code. The sales-tax mechanism itself was regressive, so the position should not be generalized into a complete economic ideology.

Fields also sponsored HB532 in 2010, creating a highly conditioned route for certain first-time offenders serving life without parole to seek sentence reduction and eventual parole consideration after at least 20 years. Republicans attacked the proposal as releasing murderers. The surviving detailed defense is openly partisan, so this episode is retained as campaign context rather than converted into a scored ideological dimension.

## Competing explanation

Fields had an exceptional local biography. He grew up in Cullman County, worked for the Alabama Department of Industrial Relations for decades, served as a Methodist minister, and had already won the district in a 2008 special election. A February 2010 *New York Times Magazine* profile focused on his acceptance among white constituents in a county with a notorious racial history.

His Republican opponent, Mac Buttram, was also a Methodist minister and had participated in Fields's prayer-breakfast group. The campaign therefore involved unusually personal religious and reputational dynamics. A Republican television attack over HB532 may have harmed Fields, meaning opponent messaging is not an explanation for his overperformance.

## Interpretation

Fields fits the district-congruent differentiation thesis better than a simple moderation account. His roll-call record was somewhat conservative relative to his caucus, but his documented education position favored higher public revenue, and his strongest distinctive asset was a deeply local identity capable of crossing an extraordinary racial and partisan divide. The case suggests that perceived membership in the community can be a form of candidate valence separate from ideological centrism.
