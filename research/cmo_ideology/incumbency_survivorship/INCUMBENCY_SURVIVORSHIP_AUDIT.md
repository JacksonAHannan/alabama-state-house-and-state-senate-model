# Incumbency and survivorship in Democratic CMO

Incumbency is an endogenous state: candidates must win and continue running to enter the observed incumbent pool. These diagnostics distinguish cross-sectional incumbent differences from within-person transitions.

## Cross-sectional pattern

In contested races, incumbent Democrats average **21.67** points of raw overperformance versus **9.73** for nonincumbents—a raw gap of **11.95** points. But residual out-of-fold CMO averages **-2.07** for incumbents versus **-0.23** for nonincumbents.

## Selection into office

At the first observed contested nonincumbent race, winners average **26.08** raw overperformance and **7.56** residual CMO; losers average **-4.00** and **-6.95**, respectively. Winning therefore selects an unusually strong-performance subset into the potential incumbent pool.

## Within-person transition

There are **29** observed Democrats who win a contested race as a nonincumbent and appear four years later as an incumbent. **7** are unopposed at the next election; challenger deterrence is itself a possible incumbency effect.
Among the **22** who face another two-party contest, raw overperformance changes by **-12.01** points and residual CMO by **-11.83** points on average. The residual decline has p = **0.0059**.

## Interpretation

The data are consistent with substantial survivorship and regression-to-the-mean. The large cross-sectional raw incumbent premium should not be interpreted as a causal incumbency effect. At the same time, conditioning on a subsequent contested race excludes incumbents who deter challengers and can bias a transition estimate downward. A structural model should treat winning, rerunning, challenger entry, and vote performance as linked stages rather than placing a single exogenous incumbent dummy in the outcome equation.
